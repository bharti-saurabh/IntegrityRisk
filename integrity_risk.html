Cardholder fraud is a stranger using a card. Integrity risk is the merchant itself being something other than what the file says. It hides in codes, tickets, MIDs and statement strings, and it clears authorization every time.

Five anatomies below

Nothing here is a declined transaction.

Every pattern on this page approves. The card is valid, the cardholder is real, the funds move, the merchant is boarded. Fraud controls look at the transaction and find nothing wrong with it, because nothing is wrong with it.

What's wrong is the description. A category code that doesn't match the goods. One sale wearing six receipts. A merchant ID carrying somebody else's business. A statement line the cardholder can't place. A purchase that hands over cash.

Integrity risk is only visible when you compare the declared record against observed behavior. That comparison is the seam, and it runs through all five.

Fraud monitoring asks
Is this transaction real?
Card, device, velocity, geography, cardholder history. Answers in milliseconds, at the transaction.
Integrity monitoring asks
Is this merchant what it claims?
Category, ticket shape, ownership, descriptor, fulfilment. Answers over weeks, at the portfolio.

Also: category drift · code shopping · misclassification

A merchant category code decides interchange, which brand rules apply, whether issuers will even authorize, and how closely anyone looks. It is chosen at boarding and rarely revisited. Behavior moves. The code stays.

No single measure is disqualifying. A restaurant can trade online, and some trade late. Five measures moving together, away from 61,000 peers, is not a restaurant.

Why a merchant does it

  • Cheaper interchange. Category sets the rate. A few basis points across millions in volume is the whole motive.
  • Rule avoidance. Restricted and prohibited categories carry registration, extra fees and brand scrutiny.
  • Issuer access. Some issuers decline whole categories outright. Recoding restores approval.
  • Lower attention. A benign code keeps the file out of enhanced monitoring queues.

What gives it away

  • Ticket-size distribution against a live peer cohort, not a static rule
  • Hour-of-day and day-of-week shape versus category norm
  • Card-present versus card-not-present mix, and 3DS take-up
  • Refund and chargeback profile inconsistent with the goods
  • Website content, checkout path and descriptor text against the code
  • Cross-border share and issuer-country spread

What it costs

Every downstream control

Interchange is only the visible leak. The code is an input to fraud scoring, underwriting limits, reserve sizing and reporting. Miscode the category and every model beneath it is calibrated to the wrong business.

Why a merchant does it

  • Beat the ceiling. Authorization floor limits, per-transaction caps, step-up and manual-review triggers.
  • Beat the calendar. Daily or monthly volume caps set at underwriting.
  • Beat the report. Amounts kept below regulatory or internal reporting thresholds.
  • Rescue a decline. Sometimes it starts benignly, as a workaround for a limit, then becomes routine.

What gives it away

  • Same PAN and same MID inside a short window, repeatedly
  • Amounts that are even divisions, or that sum to round figures
  • Sequential or near-sequential authorization codes
  • Amount histogram piling up just beneath a known threshold
  • Identical terminal, operator ID and entry mode across the set
  • Cardholders disputing part of a set they only remember as one purchase

What it costs

Your limits stop being limits

Exposure is understated across the whole portfolio because it is measured per transaction. Disputes rise, since a cardholder who bought one thing sees six lines and recognizes none of them.

Why it happens

  • Demand from the unbankable. Prohibited, sanctioned, terminated or MATCH-listed businesses still need to accept cards.
  • A rentable asset. A boarded MID with clean history is worth real money to sell access to.
  • Distance. Sub-merchants, marketplaces and payment facilitators put layers between the acquirer and the seller.
  • Time. Underwriting happens once. The business can change the week after.

What gives it away

  • Volume step-change with no matching marketing, seasonality or store openings
  • Cardholder geography that has nothing to do with the merchant's footprint
  • Checkout pages and payment URLs hosted off the merchant's own domain
  • Device and IP clusters shared with unrelated merchant files
  • Bimodal ticket distribution: two businesses, two shapes, one MID
  • Chargeback reason codes drifting toward goods never received or not as described

What it costs

Liability without knowledge

The acquirer holds settlement risk for a business it never assessed, and brand, sanctions and prohibited-goods exposure for content it never saw. When the sportsbook fails, the chargebacks arrive against the garden supply.

Descriptor Intelligence

Twenty-five characters decide whether anyone objects.

Also: statement descriptor · soft descriptor · billing string

The descriptor is the only part of a merchant a cardholder ever reads. Make it clear and disputes fall. Make it unreadable and the cardholder cannot name what to dispute. Sometimes that's carelessness. Often it is the point.

An unreadable descriptor produces two opposite outcomes at once: disputes the merchant never has to answer, because nobody can name the charge, and disputes the issuer cannot resolve, because nobody can name the charge.

Why it matters more than it looks

  • It is the mask for factoring. A sub-merchant's traffic passes as the host's the moment the descriptor says so.
  • It manufactures first-party disputes. Unrecognized charges are disputed by honest people.
  • It buys time. Rotate the string and the pattern-matching that connects a merchant to its history breaks.
  • It is cheap to change. No boarding, no review, often just a settings field.

What gives it away

  • Descriptor string that shares no tokens with the legal or trading name
  • Numeric suffixes that increment, rotate or reset by cycle
  • Edit frequency, and edits timed within days of a chargeback event
  • One descriptor family spanning several unrelated MIDs
  • Concentration of unrecognized-transaction dispute codes
  • Descriptor inconsistent with the MCC, the website and the checkout brand

What it costs

Trust, at the only touchpoint you have

Dispute handling, issuer call volume and write-offs all rise. Worse, the descriptor is the thread that ties a transaction back to a real business. Cut it and every other investigation on this page gets harder.

Cash-Disbursement

The goods never existed. The money left anyway.

Also: quasi-cash · cash-out · disguised cash advance

A card is swiped for merchandise. No merchandise moves. The cardholder walks out with cash, chips, a wallet balance or crypto, minus a fee. Coded as a purchase, it dodges cash-advance pricing, cash-advance limits and every rule written for handing out money.

The merchant keeps $120 as the fee. The issuer priced this as a purchase, so it carries no cash-advance rate, no cash-advance limit and no cash-advance flag. The credit line has been converted to cash without anyone recording that it was.

Why it happens

  • The cardholder wants cash at purchase pricing instead of cash-advance pricing, and past the cash limit.
  • The merchant wants the fee and books volume without holding stock.
  • Someone wants the trail broken. Card in, cash out, is a laundering step with a legitimate-looking receipt.
  • A bust-out is being staged. Lines are drawn down fully, then abandoned.

What gives it away

  • Round amounts, and amounts landing just under the available credit line
  • Sales volume with no matching inventory, shipping or fulfilment record
  • Same card, same merchant, repeated high-value visits in a short period
  • Approval rates and ticket sizes far above the category norm
  • Authorization patterns that mirror ATM behavior inside a retail MCC
  • Rapid utilization increase across a cohort of cards sharing one merchant

What it costs

Credit loss and an AML exposure

The issuer carries an unsecured cash balance it never priced as cash. If the cards are part of a bust-out, the loss lands in full. And a merchant converting cards to currency at scale is a money services business that nobody licensed.
